1638–1687 · Mechanics

Motion needs a cause only when it changes.

Mass, velocity, acceleration and force provide a language for physical motion.

GoalTrace a measurement from position to acceleration, then connect force to momentum.

The ramp

A wooden beam about 12 cubits long and half a cubit wide holds a straight, polished groove. Parchment lines the groove; a smooth bronze sphere rolls down it.

The clock

A raised water vessel feeds a thin spout. An operator opens and closes the outlet during each run, then weighs the collected water on a balance.

Dimensions in the source

The original note approximates the 12-cubit beam as 5.5 m and illustrates a 1–5° incline. Cubit conversions and historical apparatus details require a stated convention.

Why slow the fall?

A freely falling object moves too quickly for Galileo’s timing method to measure short intervals accurately. A shallow incline stretches the elapsed time while preserving a measurable acceleration.

Galileo’s challenge

Aristotelian accounts associated faster falling with heavier bodies and maintained that continued motion required a mover. Galileo tested and analysed motion instead.

The reported pattern

Distance from rest grows with the square of elapsed time. The original note compares heavier and lighter spheres; actual rolling depends on shape and experimental conditions.

The 1638 account

Galileo described these studies in Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to Two New Sciences.

Mass and free fall

In the ideal near-Earth model without air resistance, gravitational acceleration is independent of the falling object’s mass. Rolling down a ramp adds rotational dynamics.

Inertia · a thought experiment

In an ideal double ramp, a rolling sphere approaches its release height on the opposite ramp. Flatten that ramp and the limiting motion continues without a sustained push.

Electric fields influence carriers in a crystal. Device transport also requires band structure and scattering, which this page introduces later.

Effective mass

In a crystal, the curvature of an energy band controls a carrier’s response to an applied force. The resulting effective mass is a band property, not the free-electron mass in every direction.

The caloric problem

The source contrasts caloric theory with heat produced by friction. Rumford’s cannon-boring observations in 1798 raised questions about heat as an indestructible fluid.

Inside the vessel

A brass wheel with eight sets of revolving arms passes between four sets of fixed vanes. The vanes increase shearing of the water.

The repeated drop

The source describes two lead weights, about 29 lb each, falling roughly 63 inches. The weights were raised and dropped repeatedly, about 20 times.

Thermometry

Joule described a thermometer readable to 1/200 °F under his measurement method. The experiment linked a small temperature rise to measured mechanical work.

Corrected · Experimental losses

Real pulleys and cords have friction, and the vessel can exchange heat with its surroundings. A quantitative energy balance must account for these effects. Joule’s 1850 paper.

Potential energy

Near Earth’s surface, U = mgh relative to a selected height. In a radial field, U = −GMm/r when zero is at infinity.

Potential per mass

Gravitational potential is U/m = −GM/r. Electric potential later uses energy per unit charge.

F = −∇U A conservative force points in the direction of decreasing potential energy.
Bridge to the chip

A capacitor at voltage V stores ½CV². Circuit switching transfers energy, and resistive paths dissipate some of it as heat.

Power density

The source uses a 350 W server processor as a heat-dissipation example. Actual power and heat flux depend on the chip and operating conditions.

Added · distinct V² relationships

The squared speed in kinetic energy and the squared voltage in capacitor energy are separate physical relationships. Both support energy accounting; neither formula derives directly from the other.

Corrected · Solar comparison

The source compares 100 W/cm² with the Sun’s surface. NASA reports roughly 6,294 W/cm² of solar surface emission, about 63 times higher. NASA Sun fact sheet.

Corrected · Heat

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. The water’s internal energy rises when the paddles do work.

Historical observations

Galvani saw frog muscle respond to electrical stimulation. Volta also described a metallic taste when different metals contacted the tongue and an external conductor.

Milestones

The source places Galvani’s observations around 1780, Volta’s tongue test in 1792 and the pile in 1800. Ohm’s thermocouple measurements followed in 1826–1827.

A complete voltaic cell derives electrical energy from electrochemical reactions. Contact between dissimilar metals alone does not explain the sustained output.

Ohm used a thermocouple with hot and cold junctions. The source describes boiling water and melting ice as reference temperatures.

Early chemical batteries could vary under load. A temperature difference offered Ohm a more repeatable source for comparing conductors.

10.1 · Three storage mechanisms

Speed, density and retention trade off.

SRAM

Two cross-coupled inverters reinforce one of two voltage states. A typical 6T cell needs power but no periodic refresh.

DRAM

One access transistor connects a storage capacitor to a bitline. Charge leaks, so the cell needs refresh.

Flash

Trapped charge shifts a transistor’s threshold voltage. A read compares cell conduction with a reference.

SRAM read and write

Precharged bitlines connect to the two storage nodes when the wordline opens. A sense amplifier detects their difference. A write driver forces the desired state through the access transistors.

Write a bit

Drivers place opposite values on the bitlines. The wordline connects the drivers to the cell, allowing feedback to settle to the new state.

Write sizing

For a write-zero, the access path must pull the node past the opposing inverter’s switching point. Access and pull-up strengths influence write ability.

12.0 · Scaling limits

Smaller transistors need stronger electrostatic control.

Classical constant-field scaling connects dimensions, voltages, speed and power density under specific assumptions.

Gate leakage

A very thin dielectric allows quantum tunnelling. This adds unwanted current through the gate stack.

Drain-induced barrier lowering

At short channel lengths, the drain field can lower the source barrier. The gate then has less control over off-state current.

Historical oxide example

The source cites a silicon-dioxide gate layer about 1.2 nm thick. Thin barriers can increase tunnelling; the exact leakage depends on the stack and bias.

Dennard scaling

Constant-field scaling relates transistor dimensions, supply voltage and electric field. Leakage and voltage-scaling limits made the original power-density trend harder to sustain.

Corrected · Scaling

Scaling limits emerged gradually and depend on device design. A process-node name is not itself the channel length or an exact leakage threshold.

Growing the boule

A seed crystal contacts the melt and is pulled upward under controlled conditions. The growing solid inherits the crystal orientation; the boule is sliced and polished into wafers.

Czochralski · 1916

A familiar account describes Czochralski dipping a pen into molten tin and drawing out a thin thread. Treat the pen incident as an anecdote; the documented method studied crystal growth from a melt.

Materials and motion

High-purity silicon is melted in a crucible. The seed and crucible can rotate while the seed is pulled to control growth.

Wafer preparation

The solid boule is sliced and polished. A 300 mm wafer is one common example; diameter, thickness and surface specifications depend on use.

Illustrative process values

The source lists 99.9999999% silicon purity, a 1–2 mm/min pull rate and a 775 µm wafer thickness. These are examples, not universal process settings.

The source describes every boule as perfectly defect-free. Crystal growth controls defects, but real materials and production processes have measurable limits.

Not every silicon wafer uses Czochralski growth. Other methods, including float-zone growth, also exist.
